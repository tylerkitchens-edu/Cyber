Wireless Networking Basics

Wireless networking connects devices without cables. Laptops, phones and IoT devices use technologies such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular (4G LTE and 5G) and NFC. All of them send data over radio frequency (RF) signals.

Wi-Fi uses three frequency bands. 2.4 GHz reaches farther and goes through walls better, but it is crowded (microwaves, Bluetooth and neighbors' networks) and has only three channels that don't overlap: 1, 6 and 11. 5 GHz is faster and has many more channels, but shorter range. 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7) adds a large, clean band for newer devices, with the shortest range of the three.

Signal quality is measured with signal strength (RSSI, in dBm: -50 is strong, -80 is weak) and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Wider channels (40, 80, 160 or 320 MHz) carry more data but use up more of the band. Technicians run a site survey with a Wi-Fi analyzer and produce a heat map to decide where access points go and which channels they use.

How wireless networks are built

  • SSID: the network name people see. BSSID: the MAC address of one access point's radio. ESSID: the same SSID shared by many access points so devices can roam between them.
  • Infrastructure mode: devices connect through an access point (normal for homes and offices). Ad hoc: devices connect directly to each other. Mesh: access points connect to each other wirelessly to cover a large area.
  • Autonomous vs lightweight access points: an autonomous access point is configured on its own; lightweight access points are managed together by a wireless LAN controller (on site or in the cloud).
  • Antennas: omnidirectional antennas send in all directions (most access points); directional antennas (Yagi, parabolic) focus the signal in one direction, for example to link two buildings.
  • Cellular: 4G LTE and 5G connect phones, hotspots and backup internet links through a mobile carrier. 5G adds higher speeds and much lower latency.

Wireless Security

Wireless signals travel through open air, so anyone nearby can try to listen or connect. Encryption and authentication are what keep them out.

ProtocolEncryptionUse it?
WEPRC4Never. It can be cracked in minutes.
WPATKIPNo. Retired.
WPA2AES (CCMP)Acceptable with a long, random passphrase. A weak passphrase can be guessed offline after capturing the handshake.
WPA3AES (CCMP; GCMP-256 in 192-bit mode), SAE handshakeBest choice. SAE stops offline guessing of the passphrase, and Enterprise mode offers 192-bit security.
  • Personal (PSK / SAE): everyone shares one passphrase. Fine for homes and small offices.
  • Enterprise (802.1X): each user signs in with their own account, checked by a RADIUS server (often using EAP). When someone leaves, you disable their account instead of changing the password for everyone.
  • Guest networks and captive portals: put visitors on a separate network or VLAN, often behind a web page where they accept terms or sign in.
  • Turn off WPS. Its PIN can be guessed.
  • Hiding the SSID and MAC filtering are not real security. The SSID still appears in traffic, and MAC addresses are easy to copy. Use them only on top of strong encryption, never instead of it.
  • Watch for rogue access points (plugged into your network without permission) and evil twins (fake access points copying your SSID to capture logins).

Check your understanding

An office's 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi is slow because neighbors' networks overlap. Which channels should the access points use?

In the 2.4 GHz band only channels 1, 6 and 11 don't overlap. Moving clients to 5 GHz or 6 GHz also helps.

A company of 200 people wants every employee to sign in to Wi-Fi with their own account, so access ends when someone leaves. What should it use?

Enterprise mode checks each user's own credentials against a RADIUS server. Personal mode uses one shared passphrase, and hiding the SSID or filtering MACs doesn't stop a determined attacker.

Try it on your computer (Windows, 5 minutes)

  1. Connect to Wi-Fi, open Command Prompt and run netsh wlan show interfaces.
  2. Find these lines: Radio type (which 802.11 standard), Band and Channel, Authentication (WPA2 or WPA3, Personal or Enterprise) and Signal.
  3. Run netsh wlan show networks mode=bssid to see the networks around you and the channels they use. Are any on the same 2.4 GHz channel as yours?

On a Mac, hold Option and click the Wi-Fi icon to see the same details.

Introduction to VLANs and VPNs

VLANs

A VLAN (Virtual LAN) splits one physical switch network into several separate logical networks. A company can keep HR, Finance and guest Wi-Fi traffic apart even when they share the same switches. Each VLAN is its own broadcast domain, so broadcasts stay smaller and devices in one VLAN can't reach another without passing through a router or firewall. Links that carry several VLANs between switches are called trunks and use IEEE 802.1Q tags.

VPNs

A VPN (Virtual Private Network) builds an encrypted tunnel across a public network such as the internet, using protocols like IPsec or SSL/TLS. A site-to-site VPN links two offices. A remote-access VPN (client-to-site) connects one user's device to the company network. Public Wi-Fi is a common reason to use one.

Network Security Essentials

Networks are protected in layers (defense in depth): firewalls control traffic, IDS/IPS detect and block attacks, encryption protects data in transit, multifactor authentication protects accounts, and regular audits check the controls still work. Phishing and other social engineering remain the most common way in, so user training matters as much as technology. You'll study these controls in depth in the Network Security course.

Common Network Ports

Ports identify which service on a device should receive the traffic. They belong to the transport layer (layer 4) and are used by TCP or UDP. Firewalls are configured by port, so knowing the common ones is a daily skill.
