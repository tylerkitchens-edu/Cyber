Lesson 4: Implement Identity and Access Management

CompTIA Security+ (SY0-801)

Overview

Most breaches start with a stolen or misused account. Identity and access management (IAM) is how an organization makes sure every account belongs to the right person and can do only what it should.

You will learn:

  • the account life cycle: identity proofing, provisioning, access reviews and deprovisioning;
  • multifactor authentication, passkeys and passwordless sign-in, and password best practices;
  • access control models: role-based, rule-based, mandatory, discretionary, time-based and just-in-time;
  • single sign-on and federation (SAML, OAuth, LDAP), and privileged and service accounts.

On the exam this is mostly objective 4.5 (identity and access management) and the identity part of 3.2.

Step 3: Key ideas to remember

The account life cycle

  • Identity proofing: confirm the person is who they say (ID documents, HR records) before creating the account.
  • Provisioning: create the account with just the permissions the role needs.
  • Access review: regularly check who has what. People who change jobs collect permissions they no longer need: that is privilege creep.
  • Deprovisioning: disable the account the day someone leaves. Stale accounts are a favourite way in.

Authentication factors

FactorExamples
Something you knowPassword, PIN
Something you haveHard token (a key fob), soft token (an authenticator app), a one-time password (OTP)
Something you areFingerprint, face (biometrics)

MFA means two or more different factors; a password plus a PIN is still one factor. Passkeys replace the password with a key pair stored on your device, which can't be phished. Keep backup codes for when a device is lost. Good password practice today favours length over complexity, no reuse, a password manager, and checking for compromised credentials.

Access control models

ModelWho decides
Role-based (RBAC)Permissions go to roles (Nurse, Payroll); people get roles
Rule-basedRules apply to everyone, such as "no sign-ins from outside the country"
Mandatory (MAC)Labels (Secret, Top Secret) and clearances, set by the system; used by governments
Discretionary (DAC)The owner of a file decides who can use it
Time-based and just-in-timeAccess only during set hours, or granted for a short task and then removed automatically

One sign-in for many systems

  • Single sign-on (SSO): sign in once, use many applications.
  • Federation: trust another organization's sign-in, as with "Sign in with Microsoft" at a partner site.
  • SAML passes sign-in assertions between organizations; OAuth lets one app use another on your behalf without your password; LDAP is how applications query a directory such as Active Directory.
  • Privileged accounts (administrators) need the strongest protection; service accounts run software and should never be used by people (Windows gMSAs rotate their passwords automatically); emergency access ("break glass") accounts are locked away and monitored.

Check your understanding

An employee moved departments three times and still has access to systems from every old job. What is this called?

Permissions pile up as people move. Regular access reviews catch and remove them.

Which sign-in combination is true multifactor authentication?

A PIN is something you know and a fingerprint is something you are: two different factors. The others are all "something you know".

A hospital gives every nurse the same permissions by putting them in a Nurse group. Which access control model is this?

Permissions are attached to the role, and people are given the role.
