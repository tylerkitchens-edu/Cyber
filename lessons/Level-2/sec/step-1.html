Cybersecurity Fundamentals

Lesson

Learning Objectives

  • Explain the CIA triad: confidentiality, integrity and availability
  • Recognize common attacks: password attacks, interception, on-path attacks, keyloggers and social engineering
  • Identify the main types of regulated data and the rules that protect them (PCI DSS, HIPAA, FERPA, CCPA, GDPR)

The CIA Triad

The CIA triad is the best-known model in information security. It names the three things security protects. They depend on one another, and every security control supports at least one of them. (You may also see it written as AIC, with availability first; it's the same model.)

  • Confidentiality: only authorized people can see the information. Protected with access controls, encryption and secure protocols.
  • Integrity: the information is accurate and hasn't been changed without permission. Protected with permissions, hashing, digital signatures and backups. Malware, tampering and simple human error can all break integrity.
  • Availability: authorized people can reach the information and systems when they need them. Protected with redundancy, backups, patching and protection against denial-of-service attacks.

A flood of traffic takes a company's online store offline for six hours. No data was stolen or changed. Which part of the CIA triad was attacked?

Customers couldn't reach the store, so availability was lost. This is a denial-of-service (DoS) attack.

Password attacks

Dictionary attacks

A dictionary attack tries a list of likely passwords: common words, names, and passwords leaked in earlier data breaches, often with numbers or symbols added the way people usually add them ("Summer2025!"). Attackers don't need to try every word in the language: lists of millions of real leaked passwords crack weak passwords quickly.

Brute force attacks

A brute force attack tries every possible combination: aaaaaaaa, then aaaaaaab, and so on. The number of combinations grows fast with length. An 8-character password using upper- and lowercase letters and digits has 628, about 218 trillion, combinations; each extra character multiplies that by 62. That's why length matters more than anything else in a password.

Defenses: long passwords or passphrases, multifactor authentication, and account lockout or rate limiting after several failed attempts. Lockout only stops guessing against a live login page. If attackers steal a file of stored password hashes, they can guess offline as fast as their hardware allows, which is why stored passwords must be properly hashed and why long passwords still matter.

Other attacks to know

  • Traffic interception (sniffing): capturing network traffic to read passwords and data. Easy when the traffic isn't encrypted; HTTPS and other encrypted protocols defeat it.
  • On-path attack (older name: man-in-the-middle): the attacker secretly sits between two devices, for example through a fake Wi-Fi hotspot, and relays, reads or changes what they send each other. CompTIA uses the name "on-path".
  • Spoofing: pretending to be a trusted website, email sender or device. A fake login page that collects passwords is spoofing used for phishing.
  • Keylogger: malware or a small hardware device that records every key pressed, including passwords.

Social engineering

Social engineering attacks the person instead of the computer: the attacker tricks someone into giving away information or access. Forms you need to know:

  • Phishing: fake emails pretending to be a trusted company. Spear phishing targets one person or group; whaling targets executives. Vishing is by phone, smishing by text message.
  • Impersonation: pretending to be IT support, a manager or a delivery driver.
  • Shoulder surfing: watching someone type a password. Tailgating and piggybacking: following someone through a secure door. Dumpster diving: searching trash for useful papers.
  • Evil twin: a fake Wi-Fi access point named like a real one.

Warning signs in a phishing message: urgency ("your account will be closed today"), a sender address that's slightly wrong (such as micros0ft.com), links whose real address doesn't match the text, and requests for passwords or payment. When in doubt, contact the company through its official website or phone number, not the details in the message. Legitimate companies don't ask for your password by email.

Video: Professor Messer, "Social Engineering – CompTIA A+ 220-1202 – 2.5" (free A+ course).

At a coffee shop, an attacker sets up a hotspot called "CoffeeShop_Free", relays users' traffic to the internet and reads it on the way. What is this?

The fake hotspot is an evil twin, and sitting between users and the internet to read their traffic is an on-path (man-in-the-middle) attack.

A caller says they're from the help desk and asks an employee to read out the code just sent to their phone. What kind of attack is this?

It's social engineering by phone: vishing. Smishing uses text messages. Real help desks never ask for your sign-in codes.

Regulated data

Some kinds of data are protected by law or by industry rules. Organizations that hold them must protect them, and a breach can bring large fines.

  • Personally identifiable information (PII): any data that can identify a person, such as name, Social Security number, address, phone number or email address.
  • Protected health information (PHI): medical records and health data.
  • Payment card data: card numbers, expiry dates and security codes.
  • Government data: information classified or controlled by government rules.

Payment cards: PCI DSS

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard (PCI DSS) is set by the major card companies. Any organization that stores, processes or sends card data must follow it. It has 12 requirements under six goals. The current version is 4.0.1.

GoalRequirements
Build and maintain a secure network and systems1. Install and maintain network security controls (such as firewalls). 2. Apply secure configurations to all systems (no vendor default passwords).
Protect account data3. Protect stored account data. 4. Use strong encryption when sending cardholder data over open, public networks.
Maintain a vulnerability management program5. Protect all systems and networks from malicious software. 6. Develop and maintain secure systems and software.
Implement strong access control measures7. Restrict access to cardholder data by business need to know. 8. Identify users and authenticate their access. 9. Restrict physical access to cardholder data.
Regularly monitor and test networks10. Log and monitor all access to systems and cardholder data. 11. Test the security of systems and networks regularly.
Maintain an information security policy12. Support information security with organizational policies and programs.

Health information: HIPAA

In the United States, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) sets the rules for health information held by healthcare providers, health plans and the companies that work for them. Two parts matter most to IT:

  • The Privacy Rule protects health information in any form: paper, spoken or electronic.
  • The Security Rule sets the safeguards for electronic health information (ePHI): administrative, physical and technical.

Other laws to know

LawWhat it does
Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA), United StatesProtects student education records at schools that receive U.S. Department of Education funding. Students (or parents of younger students) can see and ask to correct their records, and schools generally need written consent before sharing them.
California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), amended by the California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA)In effect since 2020. Gives California residents rights over the personal information businesses collect about them: to know what is collected, to delete it, to correct it, and to opt out of its sale or sharing.
General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), European UnionIn effect since 2018. Protects the personal data of people in the EU, and applies to any organization in the world that handles their data. It gives people rights such as access, correction and deletion ("the right to be forgotten") and allows fines of up to 4% of a company's worldwide yearly revenue. Many companies give these rights to all their users.

An online store keeps customers' credit card numbers so they can check out faster. Which standard must it follow?

PCI DSS applies to anyone who stores, processes or sends payment card data. HIPAA covers health data, and FERPA covers student records.

  2. Find a phishing email in your spam folder (don't click its links or open attachments). List the warning signs you can spot: sender address, urgency, link addresses (hover to see them), requests for information.
