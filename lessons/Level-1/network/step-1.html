Computer Networking Basics

What is a network? In computing, a network refers to a group of interconnected devices, such as computers, servers, routers, switches, and other hardware, that are able to communicate with each other and share resources. The purpose of a network is to enable the sharing of information, data, and resources between the devices connected to the network.

To create a network, four things are required:

  1. Computers: Any computers themselves, including desktops, laptops, mobile devices, and servers.
  2. Network Media: The physical or wireless channels that allow data to be transmitted between devices on a network.
  3. Network Interfaces: Devices installed on each computer that can send and receive signals through the network media.
  4. Network Protocols: The rules and standards that govern how computers send and receive data on the network.

Network Types

There are several types of computer networks, classified by size, geographical area, and purpose:

  • LAN (Local Area Network): Covers a small area such as a building or campus. Connects devices for file sharing and communication.
  • WAN (Wide Area Network): Spans large areas such as cities or countries, connecting multiple LANs — the Internet is the largest WAN.
  • MAN (Metropolitan Area Network): Covers a city or town, often run by governments or large organizations for high-speed data transfer.
  • PAN (Personal Area Network): Connects devices close to a person, such as phones, tablets, and laptops, often using Bluetooth or NFC.
  • VPN (Virtual Private Network): Provides secure remote connections to a private network through encryption over the Internet.
  • SAN (Storage Area Network): Connects storage devices to servers for centralized storage and data management.

Public vs Private Networks

Networks can be either public or private. A private network is restricted to authorized users within the organization, providing internal file sharing and security. A public network is open for general access, such as the Internet, which interconnects millions of networks worldwide.

IEEE 802 Standards

The Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) 802 standards define networking protocols for LANs and wireless networks. The 802.11 standard governs Wi-Fi technologies.

Wireless standards have evolved to improve speed, security, and reliability:

  • WEP (1997): Wired Equivalent Privacy — the first wireless encryption, now obsolete due to weaknesses.
  • WPA (2003): Wi-Fi Protected Access — used RC4 and TKIP for stronger encryption.
  • WPA2: Enhanced WPA using AES encryption, providing modern wireless security.

Devices on a Wi-Fi network use a shared SSID (Service Set Identifier) as the network name.

802.11 Standard Comparison

  1. Most modern wireless networks use 802.11n or 802.11ac.
  2. 5 GHz frequencies have 24 non-overlapping channels, while 2.4 GHz has only 3.
  3. 5 GHz provides faster speeds but shorter range due to higher attenuation.

Bluetooth Technology

Bluetooth enables short-range wireless communication between devices using low-power radio signals, forming a Personal Area Network (PAN). It supports up to eight connected devices and operates at 2.4 GHz, similar to Wi-Fi but optimized for low data transfer and energy efficiency.
