Computer Networking Basics

What is a network? A network is a group of connected devices (computers, phones, servers, printers, routers, switches) that can communicate and share resources such as files, printers and an Internet connection.

Every network needs four things:

  1. Devices: the computers, phones, servers and other equipment that send and receive data.
  2. Network media: what carries the signal: copper cable, fibre optic cable, or radio waves (wireless).
  3. Network interfaces: the hardware in each device that connects it to the media, such as a wired network card (NIC) or a wireless adapter.
  4. Protocols: the agreed rules for how devices format, send and receive data, such as TCP/IP.

Network Types

Networks are usually named by how much area they cover:

  • PAN (Personal Area Network): devices around one person, such as a phone, earbuds and a smartwatch, usually over Bluetooth.
  • LAN (Local Area Network): one home, office, building or campus. A WLAN is a wireless LAN (Wi-Fi).
  • MAN (Metropolitan Area Network): covers a city or town, often run by a city, a utility or an Internet provider.
  • WAN (Wide Area Network): connects LANs across cities or countries. The Internet is the largest WAN.
  • SAN (Storage Area Network): a fast, separate network that connects servers to shared storage.

A VPN (Virtual Private Network) is not a size of network: it is an encrypted tunnel across the Internet that makes a remote device act as if it were on the private network, for example an employee working from home.

Public vs Private Networks

A private network is limited to authorized users, such as a company's internal network. A public network is open to anyone, such as the Internet or a coffee shop's Wi-Fi. On public Wi-Fi, assume others can see your traffic unless it is encrypted (HTTPS or a VPN).

Network Topologies

A topology is the layout of how devices connect. The physical topology is how the cables or radios actually connect; the logical topology is how data flows.

  • Star: every device connects to a central switch or access point. The most common LAN layout today. One cable failing affects one device; the central device failing takes down everything.
  • Bus: all devices share one cable. Old and rare: one break stops the whole network.
  • Ring: each device connects to the next in a loop. Mostly historic, still seen in some provider fibre networks.
  • Mesh: devices connect to several others, so data has more than one path. Used for WAN links and home "mesh Wi-Fi" systems. Reliable but costly to cable.
  • Hybrid: a mix, such as several stars linked together. Most real networks are hybrids.

In an office, every PC is cabled to one switch in the closet. One PC's cable is cut and only that PC goes offline. Then the switch loses power and every PC goes offline. Which topology is this?

Every device has its own link to one central device. A single link failing affects one device; the centre failing affects all of them.

Wi-Fi: The IEEE 802.11 Standards

The IEEE (Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers) writes the 802 family of networking standards. 802.3 is wired Ethernet and 802.11 is Wi-Fi. Since Wi-Fi 6, each standard also has a simple generation name.

Real-world speeds are much lower than these maximums. New devices and routers today are mostly Wi-Fi 6, 6E or 7, and each standard works with the older ones on the same band.

Bands and channels

  • 2.4 GHz: longest range and best through walls, but slower and crowded. Only 3 channels don't overlap (1, 6 and 11 in the US), and microwaves and Bluetooth share the band.
  • 5 GHz: faster, with many more non-overlapping channels, but shorter range.
  • 6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E and 7): the fastest and least crowded, with the shortest range.

Every wireless network has a name, its SSID (Service Set Identifier).

A customer wants the best Wi-Fi signal in a back bedroom, two walls away from the router. Speed matters less than staying connected. Which band should that room's devices use?

Lower frequencies travel farther and pass through walls better. 2.4 GHz is slower but has the longest range.

Wireless Security

SettingWhat it isUse it?
OpenNo password, no encryption.Only for guest networks, never for private data.
WEP (1997)The first Wi-Fi encryption. Can be cracked in minutes.Never.
WPA (2003)A stopgap fix using TKIP. Also broken.Never.
WPA2 (2004)Strong AES encryption. Still very common.Yes, if WPA3 is not available. Use a long passphrase.
WPA3 (2018)Current standard. Replaces the pre-shared key handshake with SAE, which resists password-guessing attacks, and uses stronger encryption.Yes: the best choice.
  • Personal vs Enterprise: Personal mode uses one shared passphrase for everyone (homes, small offices). Enterprise mode gives each user their own login, checked by a central server (RADIUS, using 802.1X), so one person leaving doesn't mean changing everyone's password.
  • WPS (Wi-Fi Protected Setup): the push-button or 8-digit PIN shortcut for joining a network. The PIN can be guessed quickly, so turn WPS off.

You are setting up a new router for a small dental office. The router offers WEP, WPA2-Personal, WPA3-Personal and WPS. What should you choose?

WPA3 is the strongest option, and WPS's PIN can be guessed. Hiding the SSID does not secure anything.

Bluetooth

Bluetooth is short-range wireless for connecting devices to each other (headsets, keyboards, phones, watches), forming a PAN. It uses the 2.4 GHz band and low power. Devices are paired once, sometimes with a PIN or a code to confirm, and then reconnect automatically.

Bluetooth or Wi-Fi?

Use Bluetooth to connect one device to another nearby (earbuds to a phone) with little power. Use Wi-Fi to connect devices to a network and the Internet at higher speed and range. Keep Bluetooth devices up to date and turn off discoverable mode when not pairing; attacks such as bluesnarfing (stealing data) target old or always-discoverable devices.

Try it on your computer (Windows, 3 minutes)

  1. Connect to Wi-Fi. Open Command Prompt, type netsh wlan show interfaces and press Enter.
  2. Find Radio type (which 802.11 standard you are using), Band and Channel. Which Wi-Fi generation is that?
  3. Find Authentication. Is your network using WPA2 or WPA3, Personal or Enterprise?
  4. Optional: netsh wlan show networks mode=bssid lists nearby networks with their channel and security. Are any of them open or WEP?
